## Introduction
A nation is not a patch of ground with an economy on it. It is a people who share enough — language, law, memory, manners — that they will pay taxes for strangers, obey verdicts they dislike, and, in the last extremity, fight for one another. That shared feeling is the scarcest resource a state possesses. The fourteenth-century historian Ibn Khaldun called it asabiyyah, group solidarity, and built a whole theory of history on a single observation: dynasties rise while it is strong and fall when it is spent.

For two generations the governing classes of the West have behaved as though this resource were infinite. They have admitted newcomers at a pace and on terms that no earlier society would have thought prudent, and they have treated anyone who objected as a moral defective. Many people now call the result an invasion. The word is wrong in one respect — no army crossed the frontier; our own governments held the door — and that is precisely the indictment. This was not done to us. We did it, and we can stop doing it.

## What the Ancients Knew
The Greeks, who invented citizenship, guarded it like treasure. In 451 BC, at the height of Athenian confidence, Pericles carried a law restricting citizenship to those with two Athenian parents. Resident foreigners were welcome to trade, to pay tax, to enrich the city; they were not handed the vote. The Athenians understood a distinction we have forgotten: between living in a place and belonging to it.

Aristotle supplied the theory. In the fifth book of the Politics he lists the causes of civil strife, and among them is the admission of settlers of a different stock before the two have had time to grow together. He does not argue the point. He simply lists the cities — Sybaris, Thurii, Byzantium, Syracuse and others — where joint settlement ended in faction, expulsion or bloodshed. For him it was not a prejudice but a pattern.

Egypt learned the same lesson more than a thousand years earlier. The Hyksos, as later Egyptians called them, were long imagined as conquering charioteers. The archaeology of their capital at Avaris tells a quieter and more instructive story: generations of settlers from the Levant arrived in the Delta as traders, sailors and labourers, grew numerous, and around 1650 BC their leaders simply took power in the north as Egypt's central authority weakened. It took a century and a war of expulsion to reverse. Seven hundred years later it happened again, when the descendants of Libyan soldiers settled in the Delta produced the pharaoh Shoshenq and a dynasty of their own. No invasion in either case. Only settlement, numbers, and a state too weak or too complacent to notice what was changing beneath it.

## Rome: Absorbing versus Admitting
The honest restrictionist does not pretend Rome was closed. Rome was the greatest assimilating machine in history. Gauls sat in the Senate; Spaniards and Africans wore the purple. But look at the terms. For centuries Rome absorbed outsiders as individuals and on Roman conditions: Latin, Roman law, service under Roman officers, and citizenship earned over a lifetime.

In 376 those terms were abandoned. A Gothic people, fleeing the Huns, arrived at the Danube and asked for asylum. The emperor Valens saw recruits and taxpayers and let them in — tens of thousands at once, more than his officials could feed, disarm or disperse. Corrupt officers starved and cheated them, and within two years they were in revolt. At Adrianople in 378 they destroyed a Roman field army and killed the emperor. The treaty that followed in 382 did something Rome had never done: it left an entire people inside the empire under its own leaders, laws and arms. They were in Rome, but not of it. In 410 their king sacked the city.

Historians argue over how much weight the settlements bear in the fall of the West, and they are right to. But the contrast is hard to escape. Rome thrived while it turned foreigners into Romans. It broke when it began admitting whole nations as nations.

## The Pattern Repeats
British tradition remembers Vortigern, the king who invited Saxon mercenaries to defend him and found that his guests had sent for their cousins. The story is half legend, but the legend survived because people recognised it.

Lebanon is not legend. Its constitutional settlement of 1943 divided power among its communities according to the census of 1932. Then the numbers moved — through differential birth rates, emigration, and the arrival of Palestinian refugees and, after 1970, the armed PLO. The institutions were built for a country that no longer existed, no faction would consent to a new count, and in 1975 the most prosperous and tolerant state in the Arab world tore itself apart in a fifteen-year war that killed more than a hundred thousand people. Lebanon did not fall to a foreign army. It fell to arithmetic.

## Europe Now
None of this is ancient history to anyone who has watched western Europe since 2015.

### Parallel Societies
Denmark's government — a Social Democratic one — uses that phrase in statute, for housing estates where most residents are of non-Western background and the usual indicators of work, schooling and crime have all gone wrong. Sweden, which for decades prided itself on being the world's humanitarian conscience, now has a gang-violence problem its own prime ministers of both left and right have tied to failed integration.

### Crime
Sweden's National Council for Crime Prevention found that between 2007 and 2018 the foreign-born were two and a half times as likely as Swedes with two Swedish-born parents to be registered as crime suspects. The figure for those born in Sweden to two foreign-born parents was higher still — just over three times. That second number is the one that should end the complacency. The promise was always that the children would be Swedes. Where people arrive without the language or skills an advanced economy rewards, are housed together, and are supported indefinitely outside the labour market, the second generation does not converge. It diverges. This is not a claim about the character of any people. It is a claim about what happens when a state imports large numbers it has no plan to employ or assimilate.

### Religious Law
England and Wales have somewhere between 30 and 85 sharia councils — no one, including the government, knows the number. They have no legal authority, and defenders stress this. But the government's own independent review found women being treated in ways no British court would tolerate, in a system many of them felt unable to avoid. A parallel legal order does not need statutory force. It needs only a community in which leaving it costs more than obeying it. Alongside it has come an unofficial blasphemy code enforced by intimidation: a teacher in Batley still in hiding years after showing a cartoon in a religious-studies lesson; a teacher in France, Samuel Paty, beheaded for the same.

### Politics
In a democracy, numbers are power — that is the system working as designed, and no one should resent citizens for voting. The danger is what kind of politics large unassimilated blocs make rational. In 2015 an English election court removed the mayor of Tower Hamlets for corrupt and illegal practices, including the use of religious authority to direct votes. In 2024 a handful of MPs entered Parliament on a platform defined by a war two thousand miles away. Parties learn quickly. When votes can be gathered by community rather than by argument, politicians stop addressing citizens and start negotiating with intermediaries. That is Lebanon's road, not Britain's.

## The Case for Hardness
Why has so little been done? Because every effective measure is one that respectable opinion calls cruel, and Western governments have preferred being thought kind to being effective.

### Denmark Chose Otherwise 
Its Social Democrats concluded that a generous welfare state and an open door cannot coexist, and acted on it: temporary rather than permanent protection, return as the stated goal, family reunification restricted, citizenship made hard to earn, and estates dominated by parallel societies broken up by law. They were denounced across Europe. Through November 2025 Denmark had granted asylum to 839 people — a historic low — and half the continent's centre-left is now quietly copying the policies it once condemned.

### Defining the Case
That is what hardness means, and it is all it needs to mean. A state serious about its survival should:

- Control entry absolutely. Asylum claims processed outside the territory; no settlement for those who enter illegally; removal of failed claimants within weeks rather than years.
- Deport foreign nationals who commit serious crimes, without the decade of appeals.
- Make citizenship the end of assimilation, not a substitute for it — language, employment, a clean record and a long probation first.
- Dissolve parallel institutions. One law, for everyone, with no informal tribunal standing between a woman and the courts; no foreign-state funding of religious institutions.
- Play the politics without flinching. Renegotiate or leave treaty arrangements that make enforcement impossible. Expect to be called names. Do it anyway.

Note what is not on this list. Nothing here touches the rights of citizens, whatever their origin, and nothing here licenses cruelty to individuals. The brutality required is brutality toward our own illusions — and it is the only alternative to something worse. When lawful governments refuse to enforce borders, the demand for enforcement does not disappear. It goes looking for someone less scrupulous. Every riot, every vigilante, every extremist party in Europe is a monument to a moderate who would not act.

## Conclusion
Athens rationed citizenship and endured. Egypt and Lebanon let the ground shift under their institutions and paid in blood. Rome flourished while it made Romans and fell after it stopped trying. The lesson is not that foreigners are enemies. It is that belonging is real, that it takes time, that it has a carrying capacity — and that a nation which will not defend the terms of its own membership is not being generous. It is merely being late.
