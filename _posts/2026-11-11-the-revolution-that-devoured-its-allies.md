## February 1979
On 11 February 1979 Iran's armed forces declared themselves neutral, and a monarchy that claimed 2,500 years of history was finished. Tehran celebrated. For a year, Marxist guerrillas, liberal lawyers, bazaar merchants, unveiled university women and turbaned clerics had marched in the same crowds. They had won.

Within a decade, most of the secular people in those crowds were dead, imprisoned, exiled or silent. The men who did it were the allies they had marched beside.

This is not a story about a foreign religion conquering a country. Iran was overwhelmingly Muslim before 1979 and after it, and most of the victims were Muslims. It is a story about one disciplined political movement, Ayatollah Khomeini's Islamism, that used a broad coalition to seize the state and then destroyed the coalition. That pattern is older than Iran, and it has not retired.

## A Muslim Country for a Thousand Years
Islam did not arrive in Iran in 1979. It arrived thirteen centuries earlier. The timeline matters, because it explains why the clergy had a reach the secular opposition never matched.

- Before 651: Persia is the Sasanian Empire. Zoroastrianism is the state religion.
- 637 to 651: Arab Muslim armies conquer the empire.
- To about 750: Conversion is slow. Roughly one Iranian in ten is Muslim under the Umayyad caliphs.
- Mid-800s: About 40 per cent are Muslim.
- 900s to 1000s: Muslims become the majority. By the late eleventh century the country is overwhelmingly Muslim, and Sunni.
- 1501: Shah Ismail I founds the Safavid dynasty and makes Twelver Shia Islam the state religion, imposed by force.
- Mid-1600s: Most Iranians are Shia.
- 1979: About 99 per cent Muslim, roughly nine in ten of them Shia.

The conversion figures are the historian Richard Bulliet's estimates, as summarised in Encyclopaedia Iranica and the overview of Islam in Iran; the Shia turn is covered in the history of the Safavid conversion.

So by 1979 Iran had been majority Muslim for about a thousand years and Shia for more than three hundred. The Safavid settlement also left the Shia clergy with something no political party had: a nationwide network of mosques, seminaries and religious taxes that did not depend on the state. It was the one institution the Shah's police could not close.

What was new in 1979 was not the faith. Through all those centuries kings ruled, and clerics taught, judged and advised. No ayatollah had ever governed. Khomeini's innovation was the claim that the clergy should hold the state itself.

## A Regime almost Everyone wanted Gone
The Shah gave Iranians many reasons to oppose him, and they were not the same reasons. Britannica's account lists the main ones: the 1953 coup, backed by the CIA and MI6, that removed Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh; censorship, surveillance, illegal detention and torture; banned or sidelined opposition parties; inflation and stagnant living standards after a decade of oil-fuelled growth; and a modernisation drive, the White Revolution, that upended landowners, clerics and the rural economy.

A liberal could hate the Shah for the secret police. A Marxist could hate him as Washington's client. A cleric could hate him for land reform, votes for women and the loss of clerical control over schools and family law. A shopkeeper could hate him for prices.

That breadth made the revolution possible. It also meant the opposition agreed on exactly one thing: the Shah must go. Nobody had settled what came next.
Through 1978 the protests grew. On 8 September troops fired on demonstrators in Tehran under martial law. On 31 October oil workers shut down the industry. In January 1979 the Shah left on what was officially called a vacation. On 1 February Khomeini flew home from exile to crowds in the millions.

## The Coalition
Khomeini's clerical network supplied the mosques, the crowds and the martyrs. It did not supply the revolution alone. Beside it stood:

- The liberals and nationalists. The National Front, heirs of Mosaddegh, and Mehdi Bazargan's Liberation Movement wanted a constitution, elections and the rule of law.
- The pro-Soviet communists. The Tudeh Party had been Iran's largest left organisation for decades.
- The Marxist guerrillas. The Fedaiyan-e Khalq had fought the Shah with guns since 1971.
- The Islamic left. The Mojahedin-e Khalq (MEK) blended Shia Islam with Marxism and drew heavily on students.
- Women. Secular professionals and religious conservatives marched together. Some unveiled women put on the chador as a badge of protest against the Shah.

The secular groups did not drift into Khomeini's camp by accident. They chose it. Shortly before the uprising, Tudeh leader Noureddin Kianouri declared that the party fully backed the Ayatollah's initiatives, including his plan for a referendum on an Islamic republic. The parties of the left then supported that referendum in March.

Their reasoning ran in a straight line. The main enemy was the Shah and the United States behind him. Khomeini was the Shah's most effective enemy. Therefore Khomeini was, for now, on the right side of history. The clerics were assumed to be a passing force: old men who could rouse a crowd but could never run a modern state. Once the monarchy fell, the real contest would begin, and the organised, educated left expected to win it.

## What He Said, and What He had Written
In October 1978 Iraq expelled Khomeini, and he settled in Neauphle-le-Château, a village outside Paris. It was the best thing that ever happened to him. The world's press came to his door, and for four months he gave interview after interview.

The message was reassuring. He spoke of a republic. Women would be free to study, vote and sit in parliament. His own aides included Western-educated men such as Abolhassan Bani-Sadr, later Iran's first president, who said decades afterwards that Khomeini broke the promises he made in Paris, including that women would have the right to choose.

The plan was not hidden, though. In 1970, in exile in Najaf, Khomeini had delivered a series of lectures published as Islamic Government. They argued that in the absence of the Hidden Imam, the state should be ruled by a senior Islamic jurist. The doctrine is called velayat-e faqih, guardianship of the jurist. Anyone could read it. Few of his secular allies did, and fewer believed he meant it.

Some Western intellectuals were as charmed as the Iranian left. The French philosopher Michel Foucault travelled to Iran twice in 1978 and wrote admiringly of a new "political spirituality." He assured readers that nobody in Iran envisaged a regime in which clerics would hold a controlling role. An Iranian woman writing as Atoussa H. answered him in the French press that November. The Western left, she observed, found Islam desirable for other people.

The mask slipped fast. By mid-March 1979 Khomeini was telling an audience in Qom not to listen to people who talked about democracy.

## 1979: the Year of the Takeover
The new order arrived in steps, and each step had allies who thought it was aimed at somebody else.

### Women were First 
Within weeks the Shah-era Family Protection Law, which had given women rights in divorce and custody, was set aside as un-Islamic. On 7 March Khomeini said women in government offices should wear the hijab. The next day was International Women's Day. Tens of thousands of women filled the streets of Tehran and kept coming for six days. One of their slogans: "In the dawn of freedom, there is an absence of freedom."

They were attacked by vigilantes. The government backed off for the moment and said the Ayatollah had only recommended modest dress. But the wider public did not rally to the women, and most of the organised left declined to make their cause its own. Anti-imperialism came first. Within a few years the veil was compulsory for every woman in Iran.

### Then the Vote 
At the end of March Iranians were asked a single question: Islamic Republic, yes or no. No other form of government was on the ballot. The left campaigned for yes. The official result was 98 per cent in favour.
Then the press and the parties. In August the government shut down dozens of newspapers and banned the National Democratic Front, a liberal-left party that had protested the closures.

### Then the Liberals 
On 4 November students loyal to Khomeini seized the US embassy. Prime Minister Bazargan, the moderate face of the revolution, resigned two days later. A CIA assessment written at the time noted that the Tudeh enthusiastically supported both the embassy takeover and Khomeini's new constitution, and that Khomeini tolerated the left only because it backed him.

### Then the Constitution 
In December a second referendum approved a charter that placed a supreme clerical jurist above the president, the parliament and the army. The 1970 lectures had become the law of the land, ten months after the revolution.

## The Purge, 1980 to 1988
With the state in hand, the clerics turned on the people who had helped them take it. They did it one group at a time.

- 1980, the universities: A "Cultural Revolution" closed the campuses and purged them of secular and leftist staff and students.
- June 1981, President Bani-Sadr: Impeached. He fled to France in July.
- 1981 to 1982, the MEK, the Fedaiyan and smaller Marxist groups: Mass arrests and executions after the MEK took up arms.
- 1982, Sadegh Ghotbzadeh: Khomeini's former Paris aide and foreign minister, executed for an alleged plot.
- 1983, the Tudeh Party: Leaders and more than 1,000 members arrested. The party was banned.
- 1988, political prisoners: Thousands secretly executed in prisons nationwide.

The worst of the terror began in June 1981. After Bani-Sadr's fall, the MEK turned to armed revolt and a bomb killed 73 leaders of the ruling Islamic Republican Party. The regime answered with firing squads. According to the Library of Congress country study, fifty executions a day became routine, and Amnesty International documented 2,946 in the twelve months after the impeachment, a figure it regarded as an undercount. By the end of 1983 the leaders of the Mojahedin, the Fedaiyan and the other left groups inside Iran were dead, and thousands of their members had been executed or jailed.

The Tudeh's turn is the most instructive. The party had cheered each earlier step, including the crackdown on its rivals on the left. It was rewarded in February 1983, when its leader Kianouri was arrested along with more than a thousand members. Party leaders were put on television to confess. Many of the rank and file were shot.

The end came in the summer of 1988. On Khomeini's order, panels later known as death commissions went through the prisons. Inmates, many of whom had already served years, were asked whether they would repent of their politics and denounce their organisations. Those who gave the wrong answer were hanged and buried in unmarked graves. Amnesty International puts the minimum death toll at around 5,000. Opposition groups claim far more. Families were never told where the bodies were.

Those who escaped the gallows scattered to Paris, Berlin, London and Los Angeles. Some were hunted there too. The revolution's secular generation had been erased from Iranian public life.

## Why They got it Wrong
The Iranian left was not stupid. It included some of the best-educated people in the country. Its mistakes were ordinary ones, which is what makes them worth studying.

1. They defined themselves by their enemy. Anyone fighting the Shah and America was an ally. What that ally wanted to build was a question for later.
2. They heard the interviews and skipped the book. Khomeini's programme had been in print since 1970. His allies preferred the version he gave to foreign reporters.
3. They assumed they were the clever ones. The clerics looked like relics. The left expected to use their crowds and then inherit the state. The clerics had the same plan in reverse, and they had the mosques, the committees and the guns.
4. They treated other people's freedom as negotiable. When women marched in March 1979, they marched largely alone. The hijab was a secondary issue, they were told. It turned out to be the first brick.
5. They applauded as the others fell. The Tudeh backed the suppression of liberals, Kurds and rival leftists, expecting to be the last faction standing. It was merely the last one arrested.

None of this required a conspiracy. It required only a movement that knew exactly what it wanted, and partners who did not ask.

## The Same Pattern at the Speed of a Feed
Two features of 1979 deserve a second look before turning to the present.

The revolution's secular cadres were made on Western campuses. The number of Iranians studying abroad rose from about 4,000 in 1957 to roughly 100,000 by 1978, according to a PBS Frontline history. Their union, the Confederation of Iranian Students, was the most active organised opposition to the Shah for two decades, and its American branch joined the radical student movement of the 1960s and 1970s. Sadegh Ghotbzadeh learned his politics as a student agitator in the United States, became Khomeini's aide in Paris, and was shot by Khomeini's government in 1982.

The largest party of the left took its line from a foreign capital. The Tudeh followed Soviet foreign policy. Moscow was pleased to see a pro-American Shah fall, so the Tudeh backed the new regime from the start. The Kremlin did not invent the Iranian left's grievances. It only pointed them where it wanted them to go, and its Iranian followers paid the bill.

Campuses that radicalise the young, and foreign powers that steer the result. Both are with us now.

### The Feed 
Hostile governments run covert propaganda on American social media. This is a matter of record. Reports prepared for the Senate Intelligence Committee found that Russia's Internet Research Agency built networks of fake American activists on every major platform and drew more than 300 million engagements between 2015 and 2017. Much of its effort went into posing as Black and left-wing activists; its most popular Instagram account, a fake Black-identity page, had over 300,000 followers. In July 2024 the Director of National Intelligence announced that actors tied to Iran's government had posed as activists online, encouraged the campus protests then under way, and sent money to protesters. Tehran's aim, she said, was to "stoke discord and undermine confidence in our democratic institutions." She added that the Americans being used might not know who was on the other end.

### The Campus 
American faculties have moved sharply left within a single career. In the long-running UCLA survey, professors identifying as liberal or far left outnumbered conservatives by about 2.3 to 1 in 1989 and by 5 to 1 in 2016, while the country as a whole barely moved, according to Heterodox Academy. In a 2024 survey by the free-speech group FIRE, only 20 per cent of faculty thought a conservative colleague would be welcome in their department, and nearly half of conservative professors said they kept their opinions to themselves. A student can now complete a degree without meeting a dissenting adult.

### The Money 
Since 1986 American universities have disclosed $67.6 billion in foreign gifts and contracts. In 2025 alone the figure was $5.2 billion. The largest source was Qatar, at $1.1 billion; China gave $528 million and Saudi Arabia $285 million. These are the amounts that were reported. For decades the disclosure law went largely unenforced, and one study commissioned by the Institute for the Study of Global Antisemitism and Policy found billions more that had gone undisclosed, and reported that campuses receiving such money saw more campaigns to punish scholars for their speech and more antisemitic incidents.

Put the three together. A generation gets its politics from a feed that foreign intelligence services are known to salt. It then spends four years in institutions where one side of the argument has all but vanished, and which accept large sums from governments that jail their own dissidents. Nobody needs to write the syllabus in Doha, Beijing or Tehran. A hostile power does not have to create American radicalism. It only has to feed what is already growing, exactly as Moscow did with the Tudeh.

The student who repeats a slogan without asking who first put it in front of him is standing where the Tudeh's rank and file stood in 1979. They too were sure they were on the right side of history.

## The Warning
The Iranians who marched in 1978 wanted freedom, and they were right to want the Shah's police state gone. Their error was narrower and more common than villainy. They let the question "who are we against?" stand in for the question "what are we for?"

Four tests would have saved them. They cost nothing to apply today.

1. Read what your allies write for their own side, not what they say to yours. The programme is usually published.
2. Watch what happens to the first group they silence. In Iran it was women, in the fourth week. If you are told that group's rights can wait, yours are in the queue.
3. Ask what your ally does with dissent where it already holds power. A movement that jails its critics at home will not make an exception for its foreign admirers.
4. Be most suspicious when a cause arrives ready-made. A slogan, a villain and a crowd, delivered to your phone in thirty seconds, is how a coalition recruits people who have not asked where it is going.

In March 1979 the women of Tehran understood all of this within a month, and said so in the street. Almost nobody stood with them. Nine years later the prisons were emptied into unmarked graves.

The revolution did not betray its allies. It did what its leader had said in writing that it would do. They had simply never believed him.
