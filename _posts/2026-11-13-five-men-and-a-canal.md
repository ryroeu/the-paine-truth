## Introduction
Seventy years ago this month, British and French paratroopers held the northern mouth of the Suez Canal and Israeli tanks sat in the Sinai. The Egyptian air force had been destroyed on the ground. By any military measure the three allies had won in nine days.
Then they stopped, and within weeks they were gone. Britain's prime minister resigned in January. The Egyptian president they had set out to destroy became the most celebrated man in the Arab world. And the British and French empires, which had run much of the globe for more than a century, began to come apart.

Suez is usually told as a story about large forces: the end of empire, the price of oil, the contest between Washington and Moscow. All of that is true, and none of it explains why the crisis happened when it did and the way it did. My argument is that Suez was neither sound foreign policy nor a simple act of conquest. It was a collision between five men, each steered by his own past, his own temperament and his own interests: Gamal Abdel Nasser, Anthony Eden, Guy Mollet, Dwight Eisenhower and John Foster Dulles.

## Why a canal in Egypt belonged to Europe
The Suez Canal opened in 1869. It was a French project, paid for mostly by French investors and dug largely by forced Egyptian labor, and it cut the voyage between Europe and Asia by thousands of miles. Ships no longer had to sail around the southern tip of Africa.

Britain had opposed the canal and then found it could not live without it. It bought a large share of the canal company, and in 1882 it occupied Egypt outright. British troops stayed for seventy-four years. By the 1950s roughly two-thirds of Western Europe's oil came through the canal, which was still run by a company headquartered in Paris.

Egyptians spent those decades trying to get the British out. They finally succeeded after army officers overthrew the king in 1952 and negotiated a withdrawal. The last British soldier left in June 1956. The canal company stayed.

## How Nasser made three Enemies
Gamal Abdel Nasser was the officer behind the 1952 coup, and by 1956 he was Egypt's president. He wanted three things: a giant dam on the Nile at Aswan to bring electricity and industry to a poor country, a modern army, and the leadership of the whole Arab world. In pursuing them he fell out with the United States, Britain and France, one after another.

The Americans wanted Egypt as an ally against the Soviet Union, and Nasser could not see why he should be one. When Secretary of State John Foster Dulles came to Cairo in 1953 to make the case, Nasser pointed out that the Soviets were a thousand miles away and had never occupied an inch of Egypt, while the British had been there for seventy years. "Nobody would take me seriously if I forgot about the British," he said. The CIA then tried cash. By the account of the historians Hugh Wilford and Kai Bird, $3 million was delivered through its man in Cairo, Miles Copeland. Nasser took it as a bribe, kept it, and spent it on the Cairo Tower, which he reportedly regarded as a rude gesture pointed at the United States.

What Nasser did want from Washington was weapons. In February 1955 an Israeli raid into Gaza killed dozens of Egyptian soldiers and showed the world how weak the Egyptian army was. The United States would not sell him arms unless he committed to peace with Israel, and he refused. So that autumn he bought them from the Soviet bloc instead. In Washington this looked like Egypt drifting into the communist camp. The Americans tried to buy him back: together with Britain and the World Bank, they offered $270 million toward the Aswan dam.

The British he offended more directly. Nasser met Anthony Eden, then the British Foreign Secretary, only once, at a dinner in Cairo in February 1955. He arrived in uniform to find his host in evening dress, and came away feeling he had been treated as a beggar at a prince's table. Days later Britain backed a new military alliance built around Iraq, Egypt's rival, and Nasser set out to wreck it. Cairo's radio station, the Voice of the Arabs, urged Arabs everywhere to throw off British influence. When the young King of Jordan fired the British general who commanded his army in March 1956, Eden, by now Prime Minister, was certain Nasser was behind it. A senior Foreign Office diplomat wrote in his diary that week that London had given up on Nasser and begun looking for ways to destroy him.

The French had a simpler complaint. Since 1954 they had been fighting a rebellion in Algeria, and the rebels had Cairo's money, Cairo's radio and Cairo's blessing.

By the spring of 1956, then, two of the three Western powers already wanted Nasser gone. In May he gave the third a reason, by recognizing Communist China.

## One week in July
On 19 July 1956 Dulles told the Egyptian ambassador that the American money for the dam was withdrawn. Britain followed. Dulles and his brother Allen, the director of the CIA, had reasoned that if Moscow paid for the dam instead, the cost would strain the Soviet economy and sour its friendship with Egypt. They did not consider what else Nasser might do.

One week later Nasser stood before a huge crowd in Alexandria and announced that Egypt was taking over the Suez Canal Company. The shareholders would be paid for their shares. The tolls, from now on, would pay for the dam.

Nothing about the canal itself changed. Ships kept passing, and Egyptian pilots proved perfectly able to guide them. What changed was who held the valve on Europe's oil, and who had been made to look weak. In London and Paris the word on everyone's lips was Munich. Eden and Mollet belonged to the generation that had watched Hitler pocket one concession after another, and they cast Nasser in the same role.

## The Plot
Eisenhower told his allies plainly that the United States would not support the use of force. Three months of conferences produced nothing. So Britain and France went looking for a way to attack without appearing to start a war, and France found one in Israel.

Israel had its own reasons to fight. Egypt barred Israeli ships from the canal and from the Straits of Tiran, Israel's only outlet to the Red Sea, and raiders crossed from Egyptian-held Gaza constantly. In late October 1956, ministers from the three countries met in secret at a villa in Sèvres, outside Paris, and agreed on a script.

Israel would invade Egypt across the Sinai desert and drive toward the canal. Britain and France would then pose as peacemakers. They would order both armies to pull back from the canal, knowing Egypt could not accept, and when Egypt refused they would land troops to "separate the combatants" and take the canal for themselves. Eden wanted nothing on paper. When he learned a written agreement had been signed, he tried to have every copy destroyed.

## Nine days: the Crisis Itself
29 October. Late in the afternoon, Israeli paratroopers dropped near the Mitla Pass, deep in the Sinai and about thirty miles from the canal. Israeli armored columns crossed the border behind them.

30 October. Britain and France issued their ultimatum: both sides had twelve hours to withdraw ten miles from the canal and let Anglo-French forces occupy it. The trick was obvious to anyone with a map. Israel's army was nowhere near the canal, so the demand ordered the invader forward and the invaded backward. Israel accepted. Egypt refused. At the United Nations that day, Britain cast the first veto in its history, against an American resolution.

31 October to 4 November. British and French bombers struck Egyptian airfields and destroyed most of Nasser's air force on the ground. Nasser pulled his army out of the Sinai to defend the canal, which left the desert to Israel; within days Israeli forces held Gaza and the entire peninsula. He also did the one thing the plan had not allowed for. He sank dozens of ships in the canal and blocked it completely. The operation to keep the canal open had closed it.

Meanwhile the invasion fleet was still at sea. It had to sail from Malta, nearly a thousand miles away, and for almost a week the world watched two great powers bomb a country they claimed to be protecting. The UN General Assembly demanded a ceasefire by 64 votes to 5. In London, crowds filled Trafalgar Square to protest against their own government.

5 and 6 November. British and French paratroopers landed at Port Said, at the northern mouth of the canal, and the main force came ashore the next morning. The fighting in the city was short and one-sided, and it killed hundreds of Egyptian civilians. By the evening of 6 November the allied column had pushed about twenty-three miles down the canal, with more than three-quarters of it still ahead.

Then Eden ordered them to stop.

## Why they Stopped
Three things happened at once.

The first was Moscow. On 4 November Soviet tanks had rolled into Budapest to crush the Hungarian uprising, and the Suez invasion gave the Kremlin perfect cover. The next day the Soviet premier sent letters to London, Paris and Tel Aviv hinting at rocket attacks.

The second, and the decisive one, was money. Investors were dumping the pound, Britain's reserves were draining away, and the Arab oil that normally came through the canal had stopped. Britain needed an emergency loan and American oil. Eisenhower's Treasury Secretary refused both until the shooting stopped. Harold Macmillan, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, who had been among the keenest for war, told the cabinet that Britain could not afford to continue.

The third was the calendar. On 6 November Eisenhower was re-elected in a landslide, and whatever hope Eden had that Washington would soften after the vote was gone.

Eden telephoned Mollet, who begged for two more days to finish taking the canal. Eden refused. The ceasefire took effect at midnight. France, which had not been consulted so much as informed, never forgot it.

A United Nations force, the first armed peacekeeping mission in the organization's history, moved in to replace the invaders. The last British and French troops left Egypt three days before Christmas. Israel gave up the Sinai the following March. The canal reopened in April 1957, run by Egypt. Eden, his health broken, resigned in January.

## The End of two Empires
This is the part that matters most. Britain and France went into Suez as imperial powers and came out as something less, and everyone watching understood it.

For a century and a half the two countries had acted in Africa and Asia as they saw fit. In November 1956 they launched a war to hold on to one of the great symbols of that power, won every battle, and were ordered home within a week by their own ally and their own bankers. The lesson was drawn immediately in every colony and every capital: London and Paris could no longer impose their will by force, and Washington would not let them try.

What followed was a collapse. In 1957 Ghana became the first of Britain's African colonies to win independence. In 1960 alone seventeen African countries became independent, fourteen of them French. That same year Macmillan, now Prime Minister, told the South African parliament that a "wind of change" was blowing through the continent, which was a polite way of announcing that Britain was leaving. Within a dozen years of Suez, Britain had given up almost every colony it held and announced that it would withdraw its forces from everything east of the canal.

For France the reckoning was more violent. Mollet had gone to Egypt to win the war in Algeria. Instead the Algerian war destroyed the French Fourth Republic in 1958, brought Charles de Gaulle back to power, and ended in Algerian independence in 1962.

Neither empire fell because of Suez alone. Both were already weakened by two world wars and by nationalist movements they could not suppress. But Suez is where the pretense ended. Before it, Britain and France could still believe they were great powers. After it, no one did, including them.

## The Five Men
That is what happened, and what it cost. Why it happened is a question about people.

### Nasser: the Man who wanted more than Egypt
The best witness to Nasser's thinking is the American who delivered the briefcase. Miles Copeland stayed close to him for seventeen years, and three days after Nasser's death in September 1970 he published an account of their conversations. What drove Nasser, Copeland wrote, was humiliation. Israel was a country of two million and Egypt a country of thirty million, yet Israeli aircraft could fly over Cairo whenever they liked. Nasser compared it to Cuban planes circling Washington while the American military stood helpless.

Copeland's more surprising claim is that Nasser began with no strong feelings about Israel at all. In 1953 and 1954 he needed a common enemy to unite the Arabs behind him, and Israel served that purpose. Real hostility came later, after real defeats. In the same way, he played Washington against Moscow because he believed a man who spoke for the whole Arab world could extract more than a man who spoke for Egypt alone.

That ambition is what made him impossible to deal with. Dulles is said to have remarked that if Nasser would stick to Egypt and leave the other Arabs alone, he could have all the aid he wanted. Nasser could not. He was rational enough within Egypt's borders; even the nationalization was a way to pay for the dam from an Egyptian asset before turning to Moscow. Beyond them he managed to threaten Britain's position in Jordan and Iraq, France's in Algeria and America's Cold War design, all in the same eighteen months.

### Eden: the sick Man of Downing Street
When Eden died in 1977, The Times wrote that he was "the last Prime Minister to believe Britain was a great power" and the first to meet a crisis that proved otherwise. He had also made his name as a man of peace when he chose to resign as Foreign Secretary in 1938 rather than appease the dictators. It is hard to square that man with the one his own junior minister described.

Anthony Nutting, Minister of State at the Foreign Office, recalled sending Eden a paper in March 1956 on how Nasser might be isolated. Eden telephoned him in a rage. He did not want Nasser neutralized, he said; he wanted him destroyed, and by Nutting's account he used the word "murdered." When Nutting objected that Britain had no alternative government to put in his place, Eden answered that he did not care if Egypt fell into anarchy. Asked later about consulting Washington, he said the matter had nothing to do with the Americans. Nutting resigned when the invasion went ahead.

Part of the explanation may be medical. A botched operation in 1953 had damaged Eden's bile duct and left him prone to fevers and pain. David Owen, who was both a physician and a later Foreign Secretary, studied Eden's records and diaries and found a prime minister who through August and September 1956 was sleeping five hours a night and taking pethidine for pain. On 5 October Eden collapsed with a high fever and was hospitalized. Afterward he leaned increasingly on Drinamyl, a mix of amphetamine and barbiturate later known on British streets as "purple hearts." Its side effects include insomnia, anxiety, irritability and overconfidence.

Nine days after that collapse, he was listening to General Challe explain the plan. I cannot prove the drug made the decision. But the man Nutting describes, the man in the diaries, and the textbook description of an amphetamine user look like the same person.

### Mollet: the Prisoner of 1940 and of Algiers
Guy Mollet, a Socialist schoolteacher, became prime minister of France on 1 February 1956. In speech after speech he called Nasser a new Hitler, and he described Nasser's short book, The Philosophy of the Revolution, as a new Mein Kampf. Mollet knew the original firsthand. He had been a German prisoner of war in 1940 and 1941, then joined the Resistance and was arrested more than once by the Gestapo.

His deeper problem was a promise. Mollet had campaigned on bringing peace to Algeria, where the National Liberation Front had been in revolt since November 1954 with Cairo's radio cheering it on. Five days into his term he flew to Algiers and was pelted with tomatoes by furious French settlers. He came home a convert to war first and negotiation later, and it was a short step from there to the belief that the Algerian rebellion could be ended in Cairo. He was in London conferring with Eden about the Middle East by 11 March.

### Eisenhower: the Candidate
Eisenhower warned Eden more than once that world opinion would be outraged by an attack and that the eventual price would be too high. He was right, and his reasons were not purely moral. He had suffered a heart attack in September 1955, there had been open doubt about whether he would run again, and he was campaigning as the president who kept the peace. Saudi Arabia, America's main friend in the region, was allied with Egypt.

So through the summer and autumn of 1956 he opposed the use of force without ever making the cost of defying him concrete. One of Eden's own former aides thought the prime minister never understood how the American mind worked in an election year. Eden and Mollet seem to have assumed that a president a week from the polls would grumble and look away. When he did the opposite, they had no second plan.

### Dulles: the Man who lit the Fuse
John Foster Dulles saw the world through one lens. Communism was the enemy, and neutrality between the two camps was, in his words, "immoral." He had little experience of, or patience for, countries like Egypt that wanted help from both sides and loyalty to neither. He spent three years trying to convince Nasser that the Soviet Union, not Israel or Britain, was the real danger, and never grasped why the argument failed.

When Nasser recognized Communist China and hinted at Soviet money for the dam, Dulles took it personally. Why, he asked, should nations that play both sides be treated better than those that stand with us? Pulling the Aswan offer was partly a calculation and partly a punishment, and he and his brother were confident they knew how Nasser would respond. They were wrong within a week. Of the five men, Dulles is the one who started the clock.

## Conclusion
The Suez Crisis did not have to happen. If any one of these five men had acted differently, the invasion would probably have been avoided.

If Nasser had been content to lead Egypt, he could have had the dam on American terms and a powerful patron with it. If Eden had stepped down when his health failed, a successor with a clearer head might have listened to Washington's warnings. If Mollet had not carried the memory of 1940, or had not promised a peace in Algeria that he did not know how to deliver, France might have looked for something short of war.

If Eisenhower had spent the autumn telling his allies exactly what an invasion would cost them, instead of telling them only that he disapproved, they might not have gambled on his silence. And if the Dulles brothers had treated Nasser as a man with options of his own, they would not have bet American policy on a guess about what he would do next.

None of this required a saint. It required one of five men, at one of several moments, to look past his own grievance, his own illness, his own promise or his own election. None did. Two empires paid for it with their standing in the world, and ordinary people paid with their lives, in Port Said, in the Sinai and, for years afterward, in Algeria.

We like to explain international crises with maps and interests, because those explanations make events feel orderly. Suez is a reminder to ask a plainer set of questions first. Who is in the room? What are they afraid of? What did they promise, and to whom? How well did they sleep? Seventy years on, I would ask the same questions about any crisis in the news.
