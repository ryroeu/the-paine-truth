## Introduction
There is a story told about Franklin Roosevelt. Like most of the best stories, it may not be true.

A group of Democratic congressmen visited the White House and laid out what they wanted. Roosevelt listened and said, "Oh yes, I agree." Later a group of Republicans came and laid out what they wanted, which was close to the opposite. Roosevelt listened and said, "Oh yes, I agree." Eleanor, who had been serving refreshments at both meetings, confronted him once the room was empty: "Franklin, you can't promise both sides like that!" He thought it over and said, "Oh yes, I agree."

The joke is older than Roosevelt, and versions of it are told about rabbis and village judges. It stuck to him because it fit. Campaigning in 1932, he promised the country not a doctrine but "bold, persistent experimentation": try something, admit it frankly if it fails, and try something else. The New Deal that followed was a patchwork borrowed from progressives, conservatives, bankers and union men, and it was held together by one question only. Does it work?

That question is the whole of my argument. Leadership and law must be pragmatic. Principles tell a nation where it wants to go. They do not steer the ship, and a leader who confuses the compass with the wheel will run a free country onto the rocks while congratulating himself on his heading.

## Lincoln: the Principle and the Torch
We remember Abraham Lincoln as the Great Emancipator, and he was. We forget how he got there.

In August 1862 the editor Horace Greeley publicly demanded that Lincoln free the slaves at once. Lincoln answered in print that his "paramount object" was to save the Union, and that he would free all the slaves, some of them, or none of them, according to whichever course saved it. A draft of the Emancipation Proclamation was already in his desk. He was waiting for a battlefield victory so that it would read as strength and not desperation. Antietam gave him one, and the proclamation followed within days.

Look at what that document actually did. It was issued as a war measure under his powers as commander in chief. It freed slaves in the states in rebellion and exempted the loyal border states, because Lincoln could not afford to lose Kentucky and Maryland. A purist would have freed everyone on day one and lost the war by year two. Lincoln freed those he could, when he could, and finished the job with the Thirteenth Amendment once victory made it possible.

The same man suspended habeas corpus, let his generals shut down newspapers, and turned Sherman loose on Georgia to burn the Confederacy's capacity to fight. The end was humanitarian. The means were whatever the end required. Remove either half and slavery outlives him.

## The Long Record
Lincoln and Roosevelt were not exceptions. Go back as far as you like and the leaders who left their countries stronger were the ones who bent.

- Elizabeth I inherited an England that had burned Protestants under one monarch and persecuted Catholics under another. Her settlement of 1559 asked for outward conformity and left private conscience alone; she is credited with refusing to "make windows into men's souls." That tolerance had a limit. When the Pope declared her deposed in 1570 and released her subjects from their allegiance, priests working for her overthrow were prosecuted as traitors, not as heretics.
- Henri IV of France, a Protestant, converted to Catholicism to end thirty years of religious civil war. "Paris is well worth a Mass," he is supposed to have said. He then issued the Edict of Nantes to protect the Protestants he had just left. Neither side got everything. France got peace.
- Bismarck called politics "the art of the possible." A Prussian conservative, he built the world's first national health and pension insurance, not because he had become a socialist but because it took the socialists' best argument away from them.
- Deng Xiaoping put it most bluntly: it doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white, so long as it catches mice. A country that had starved under ideological purity became the world's second-largest economy under a man who stopped asking what Marx would think.

Four countries, four centuries, one habit of mind. Each of them asked what the situation required before asking what the doctrine allowed.

## Freedom to worship? Yes, but...
Now apply that habit of mind to the freedoms we treat as untouchable. Start with religion.

Freedom of worship is one of the great achievements of the West, and it was itself a pragmatic invention. Europe did not adopt toleration because it stopped caring about truth. It adopted toleration because a century of Catholics and Protestants killing each other had proved the alternative unworkable. The freedom exists to keep the peace. It follows that it cannot be stretched to cover what breaks the peace.

American law has said so since 1879. In Reynolds v. United States, a Mormon defendant argued that his faith required polygamy. The Supreme Court answered that government may not reach belief, but it may reach conduct, because otherwise every citizen's creed would outrank the law of the land and each man would become a law unto himself. Belief is free. A rival legal order is not.

That is the right test for Islam in the West today, and it is a test, not a verdict. Millions of Muslims live under Western constitutions with no more difficulty than their Catholic or Jewish neighbors. But there are strands of political Islam that are not merely a faith. They are a program: religious law above civil law, apostasy and blasphemy as crimes, the equal standing of women and unbelievers denied. Where a mosque, a movement, or a foreign-funded network preaches that program, or preaches violence in its service, a pragmatic state does not owe it the protection it owes to prayer.

Europe has begun to act on this. Austria's 2015 Islam law barred foreign funding of imams and mosques. France's 2021 law on "republican principles" requires religious associations to declare large foreign donations and lets the state dissolve organizations that work against the republic; the interior ministry has since inspected mosques suspected of separatism and closed some of them. Critics call these laws discriminatory, and that charge deserves an answer. The answer is the line Elizabeth I drew, as described earlier: she left private belief alone and prosecuted only those who worked with a foreign power against the state. The state has no business in the soul of the believer. It has every business with an organization that takes its money and its orders from abroad and teaches that the constitution is illegitimate. The question is never "what do you believe?" It is "what are you doing, and for whom?"

## Freedom of the press? Yes, but...
The same test applies to speech and the press.

A free press is how a republic finds its own mistakes. It is not a license to help the republic's enemies. Justice Holmes drew the line in 1919 with the man who falsely shouts fire in a crowded theater. Justice Robert Jackson, who had prosecuted the Nazis at Nuremberg and seen what they did with Weimar's freedoms, warned thirty years later against turning the Bill of Rights into "a suicide pact."

Lincoln lived by that rule. In May 1864 two New York newspapers printed a forged presidential proclamation announcing a new draft of 400,000 men. It was a hoax designed to move the gold market, in the middle of a war, in a city that had rioted over the draft the summer before. Lincoln had both papers shut and their editors arrested. During the Second World War, American newspapers and radio stations operated under an Office of Censorship and did not print troop movements or ship sailings. Nobody thinks the First Amendment died in 1942.

The modern case is the European Union's decision in March 2022, days after Russia invaded Ukraine, to take the Kremlin's RT and Sputnik off the air across the continent. Press-freedom groups objected. The EU's courts upheld the ban, on the reasoning that a state broadcaster conducting propaganda for a war of aggression is an instrument of that war and not a newspaper.

Notice what these cases share. None of them punished an opinion for being unpopular, or a paper for embarrassing the government. Each targeted a specific act, forgery, disclosure of military secrets, a hostile state's propaganda arm, during a specific danger. A news company that prints or airs what directly endangers the peace and security of the country has stepped outside what the freedom was built to protect.

## Pragmatism is not a Blank Check
The obvious objection is that every tyrant in history has claimed "peace and security." That is true, and it is the reason pragmatism has to be judged the only way a pragmatist can accept: by results.

By that standard the heavy hand has a poor record. Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685 to make France uniformly Catholic, and drove hundreds of thousands of skilled Huguenots into the arms of his rivals in England, Holland and Prussia. Bismarck spent the 1870s trying to break the Catholic Church in Germany; the Catholic party grew at every election until he gave up and made peace with it. The Federalists passed the Sedition Act in 1798 to jail hostile editors and were voted out of power for good two years later. And Roosevelt himself, the hero of my opening story, signed the order that interned 120,000 Japanese Americans on a security rationale that no evidence supported. Not one of them was ever convicted of espionage.

Those were not failures of pragmatism. They were failures to be pragmatic. Each one punished a whole people for what a few might do, and each one made the country weaker. The lesson is a short set of rules for any leader who invokes necessity:

1.  Target conduct, not identity. The organization, the funding, the act of incitement. Never the faith or the opinion as such.
2.  Demand evidence of a direct threat. A feeling of unease is not a threat. Lincoln had a forged proclamation in his hand.
3.  Keep it reviewable and temporary. Courts open, measures lifted when the danger passes.
4.  Measure the result. If the restriction produces more enemies than it removes, drop it, as Bismarck did.

## Conclusion
In seventeenth-century Japan a young samurai named Takagi Oriemon lost his father to a night ambush. According to the tradition of the school he went on to found, his father had left him one lesson: the willow is strong, while the tall tree snaps. The lesson cut close, because takagi, the family's own name, means "tall tree." Oriemon put the willow in front of his name and called his art Yoshin-ryu, the school of the willow heart. His successor, the story continues, added the image that completes it: a willow's branches can bear the snow. The stiff bough holds its load until it cracks. The willow bends, sheds the weight, and is still standing in the spring. The school, Hontai Yoshin Ryu, is still teaching nearly four centuries later.

A nation that will not bend its principles at all will eventually watch them broken by people who hold none. A nation that bends them carelessly will break them itself. The job of a leader is to stand between those two failures, which is harder than standing at either end, and wins applause from neither.

Roosevelt understood that. So did Lincoln, and Elizabeth, and Henri. They listened to everyone, promised less than they were asked, and did what the hour required. Freedom to worship? Yes. Freedom of the press? Yes. But a free country is first of all a country, and it has to survive to stay free.
