## Introduction
The men who wrote the Constitution argued for days over whether the foreign-born should govern Americans. They split the difference: a natural-born president, and a waiting period for Congress. This year Congress has been asked to finish the argument, and it should.

## The Argument in Philadelphia
The delegates of 1787 knew the English rule well. Parliament was closed to anyone born outside the kingdoms of England, Scotland or Ireland, and several delegates thought America should copy it.

On August 9, Gouverneur Morris moved that a senator be a citizen for fourteen years before taking his seat. Charles Pinckney backed him, warning of a "peculiar danger and impropriety in opening its door to those who have foreign attachments," since the Senate would make treaties. George Mason said he would have confined the Senate to natives outright, had so many foreigners not earned their place in the Revolution. Pierce Butler, born in Ireland himself, argued that a newcomer needs years to shed foreign habits and learn American law.

Four days later Elbridge Gerry went further. He wished that eligibility might in future be confined to natives, because foreign powers would meddle in American affairs and spend freely to plant their friends in American councils.

The other side was just as distinguished. James Madison thought long exclusions illiberal and likely to drive off the immigrants the country most wanted. James Wilson, born in Scotland, described what it was to be marked as lesser by the law of his adopted country. Benjamin Franklin reminded the room how many Europeans had stood with America in the war. Alexander Hamilton, born on Nevis, moved to require nothing beyond citizenship and residence, and lost by seven states to four.

Notice what the restrictionists feared. It was not the poor immigrant. It was the agent of a foreign crown: the well-connected man with European money behind him and European loyalties in his heart. The worry in that room was foreign influence over American government, and it was shared by men on both sides of the vote.

## The Compromise
The Convention settled on three tiers. A representative must have been a citizen for seven years and a senator for nine. The president must be a natural-born citizen, with an exception for those already citizens when the Constitution was adopted.
The presidential rule owes much to John Jay. In July 1787 he wrote to George Washington urging a strong check on the admission of foreigners into the administration of the national government, and asking that command of the army fall to none but a natural-born citizen. Madison later defended the nine-year rule for senators in Federalist No. 62 as a middle course between shutting out adopted citizens altogether and admitting them in haste.

So the framers accepted the principle. Birth and allegiance are legitimate qualifications for office, and the higher the office, the stricter the test. What held them back from a wider rule was the circumstance of their day. Eight of the delegates were themselves born abroad, among them Hamilton, Wilson and Robert Morris. The Revolution had been won with foreign-born officers. A republic of under four million people on the edge of a continent needed every able immigrant it could attract.
The early Senate took the rule seriously. In 1794 it unseated Albert Gallatin of Pennsylvania, born in Geneva, because he had not been a citizen for the full nine years. He went on to serve nearly thirteen years as Secretary of the Treasury, so the rule cost him a seat and not a career.

## What has changed since 1787
Naturalized citizens have sat in Congress since the First Congress, and many served with distinction. This essay does not pretend otherwise. The case for a new rule rests on something else: the reasons the framers gave for leniency have expired, and the danger they named has grown.

### The Need is Gone 
The framers held back because a small country could not spare talent. A nation of more than 330 million can fill 535 seats in Congress, some 870 federal judgeships and roughly 1,200 Senate-confirmed posts from citizens who have never owed allegiance anywhere else.

### Divided Allegiance is now Routine. 
In 1787 a man who took American citizenship generally cut his ties to the old country. Today many nations let their emigrants keep citizenship for life, and the United States tolerates it. A member of Congress may hold a second passport. A separate resolution in this Congress, H.J.Res. 194, would bar from office anyone who retains a foreign citizenship or allegiance.

### The Means of Influence have Multiplied 
Gerry worried about secret-service money from European courts. Foreign governments now cultivate their diasporas openly, fund media and lobbying, and can reach an officeholder's relatives who still live under their jurisdiction. A man with family in a foreign state has a pressure point that a native-born citizen usually lacks.

### Power has Moved 
The framers reserved the strictest test for the president because that was where they saw the greatest danger. Since then a great deal of power has passed to federal judges with life tenure, to cabinet secretaries and to committee chairmen. The Constitution already half-admits this: a naturalized Secretary of State is skipped in the line of presidential succession. If birth matters enough to bar a secretary from inheriting the office, it is fair to ask why it does not matter for the post he holds.

Britain offers a caution about divided authority, though it must be stated accurately. Sharia councils there have no legal force and Parliament has adopted no religious law. Yet a government-commissioned review in 2018 found many Muslim women relying on those councils because their religious marriages were never registered under civil law, which left them without recourse to the courts. That did not happen because of who sits in Parliament. It happened because the state never required those marriages to be registered, and so never insisted on one civil law for everyone. The lesson for America is narrow but real: questions of allegiance are far easier to settle with a clear rule at the door than to untangle afterward.

## The Amendment
Congress cannot do this by statute. The qualifications for Congress are fixed in Article I, and the Supreme Court has held that neither Congress nor the states may add to them. The only honest route is Article V: two-thirds of both houses, then ratification by three-fourths of the states.

The proposals are already on the table. In May 2026 Rep. Nancy Mace introduced H.J.Res. 188, which would require natural-born citizenship of representatives, senators, federal judges and Senate-confirmed officers. Other resolutions this Congress cover Congress alone or the judiciary alone, and an Alabama measure would apply the same test to state office.

A sound amendment would do four things:
1. Extend the natural-born requirement to Congress, the federal bench and Senate-confirmed offices.
2. Reach state office too, or expressly permit each state to adopt the same rule, so the test does not stop at the federal line.
3. Apply only going forward. Sitting officeholders finish their terms. The Mace text already works this way, and it is the right way: no one who ran under the old rules should be thrown out under new ones.
4. Define "natural born." The Constitution never has, which is why the eligibility of candidates born abroad to American parents is argued over every few elections. The amendment should say plainly that a citizen from birth qualifies.

No one should understate the difficulty. The Mace resolution had no cosponsors when last reported and sits in the Judiciary Committee. No amendment has been ratified since 1992. An argument worth making is still worth making when the votes are not yet there.

## The Objections, and the Answers
### "It creates two classes of citizen." 
This is the strongest objection. The Supreme Court said in Schneider v. Rusk (1964) that the rights of naturalized and native-born citizens are equal, with one exception: eligibility for the presidency. But that exception is the point. The Constitution has drawn this line since 1788, and an amendment that moves it is no more an assault on equal citizenship than the original clause was. Office is a public trust and not a private right. We already bar the 34-year-old from the presidency and the two-term president from a third.

### "Let the voters decide." 
That was Wilson's argument and Madison's, and it is a good one. But the Constitution does not leave every such judgment to voters. It sets ages, residency and term limits because some risks are better handled by rule than election by election.

### "It would have barred Hamilton." 
It would also have barred Wilson, Gallatin, Carl Schurz, Henry Kissinger and Madeleine Albright. Thomas Paine, whose name this site borrows, landed in Philadelphia in 1774 and was secretary to Congress's Committee for Foreign Affairs by 1777. The cost is real and should be admitted. The reply is that a qualification is a general rule, not a verdict on individuals. Some able 30-year-olds would make fine presidents, and we exclude them all the same.

### "Birthplace does not prove loyalty." 
True. Benedict Arnold was born in Connecticut. No rule screens for character. A rule can only remove one category of risk, the competing claim of another nation, and this one does.

### "It is aimed at particular people." 
Rep. Mace named three Democrats when she introduced her resolution. That was a mistake, because the rule must be neutral or it is worthless. It would bind Republicans such as Sen. Bernie Moreno and Reps. Victoria Spartz, Juan Ciscomani and Young Kim exactly as it binds anyone else. It asks one question, whether a person was a citizen at birth, and it asks it of the Scot and the Somali alike. Article VI forbids any religious test for office, and nothing proposed here touches that. An amendment argued on any ground of race, creed or country of origin would betray the framers' reasoning and would deserve to fail.

## Conclusion
The framers never settled this question; they compromised on it. They agreed that birth and allegiance are fair tests for office, applied the strictest one to the presidency, and gave Congress a waiting period because a young republic owed a debt to its foreign-born patriots and could not spare their talent. Those reasons belonged to 1787. The danger the delegates named, a foreign claim on the loyalty of those who govern, has only grown with dual citizenship, organized foreign influence and the spread of power to judges and cabinet officers. The remedy is the one the Constitution itself provides: an amendment, passed by two-thirds of Congress and ratified by three-fourths of the states, that extends the natural-born requirement to every office of public trust, applies only going forward, and asks the same single question of every candidate regardless of race, creed or country of origin. It would cost the country some able servants, as every qualification does. It would also finish the work the Convention left half done.
